0Why we started over

An older project tried to study debates but had a fundamental problem: only 41.7% of its transcript text actually matched the audio. We rebuilt from scratch in a fresh directory.

👶 5th-grade version

Imagine you have a recording of someone giving a speech and the written-down version of that speech. They should match word for word, but sometimes the written one is "cleaned up" — it skips the "um"s and "uh"s and even changes some words to sound nicer.

We tried to use those cleaned-up versions before but they didn't match the recording very well. So we threw out everything and started fresh with a better plan.

🔬 Research version

The previous pipeline relied on fuzzy text matching between Whisper ASR output and an official transcript that turned out to be heavily edited. Median match score: 57/100; only 24% of segments scored ≥80. This precluded reliable sentence-level timing for downstream prosodic analysis.

We restarted in ~/debate_analysis/ with three commitments: (1) forced alignment as the timing backbone, (2) verbatim ASR text as a fallback canonical source, and (3) per-debate fixed effects to control for era/speaker confounds.

1Collect metadata: who debated, who won

For each of 26 presidential debates from 1988 to 2024, list the candidates and label each one as "winner" or "loser" based on whether their party won the subsequent election.

👶 5th-grade version

Made a list: every presidential debate from 1988 (when George H. W. Bush was running) all the way to 2024 (Trump vs Harris). For each debate, we wrote down who was talking, their party, and whether they ended up winning the election.

So Bush is a "winner" in 1988 (he won), but a "loser" in 1992 (Clinton won that one).

🔬 Research version

Built data/metadata/debates.csv from the inventory xlsx. One row per (debate, candidate); fields: debate_audio_id, debate_id, year, debate_type, date, speaker, party, election_winner, result.

The result field is binary {winner, loser}, with the winner being whichever candidate's ticket won the presidency that election cycle. Third-party candidates (Perot, Stockdale) → loser. 76 candidate-rows across 36 debates total (26 Presidential are the focus; 10 VP debates lacked transcripts and were excluded).

2Align audio with text

Figure out, for every word in the transcript, when exactly it was spoken in the audio recording. Done using three different methods depending on transcript quality.

👶 5th-grade version

Think of subtitles on a YouTube video. Someone has to mark exactly when each word starts and ends. We had to do that for every word in every debate — about 400,000 words.

We tried 3 ways:

  • 🥇 The careful way (MFA): Worked for 20 debates. Very precise.
  • 🥈 The clever way (text matching with Whisper's auto-transcription): Worked for 4 more.
  • 🥉 The honest way (just use Whisper's auto-transcription as truth): For 2 debates from 2024 where the "official" transcript was actually a summary and didn't match the audio at all.

🔬 Research version

Hybrid 3-tier alignment cascade:

Tier A (20 debates): Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) with the english_us_arpa acoustic model and lexicon. Beam parameters tuned to 400/1000 after pilot debates showed default beams (100/400) timing out on 90-min audio. Sequential per-debate execution with 150-min watchdog (caffeinate-wrapped to prevent macOS sleep-induced false timeouts).

Tier A-equiv (4 debates): Text-based alignment via Python difflib.SequenceMatcher over normalized atom sequences. Whisper ASR words carry the timing; matching transcript words inherit those timestamps; unmatched stretches receive linearly-interpolated timing between flanks. Match rates 88.4–91.2%.

Tier B (2 debates): For 2024 broadcasts (1034, 1036) whose "transcripts" are paraphrased summaries (~5% verbatim overlap), Whisper's own ASR is treated as canonical text. Speaker labels recovered via pyannote.audio diarization clusters mapped to candidates by duration ranking within the trimmed debate window.

3Build the master sentence table

Join all the alignment outputs with the metadata into one big table: one row per sentence, with speaker, party, winner/loser label, and exact start/end times in the audio.

👶 5th-grade version

Made the world's biggest debate spreadsheet. Every row = one sentence someone said in a debate. Columns: who said it, when they started and stopped, the actual words, and whether they were a winner or a loser.

35,459 sentences in all. That's like reading a thick book of nothing but debate quotes.

🔬 Research version

Canonical sentence-level table at outputs/master_sentences.csv: 35,459 rows × 22 columns. Schema: debate_audio_id, debate_id, year, debate_type, date, turn_id, sentence_id, sentence_index_in_turn, start_sec, end_sec, duration_sec, speaker_raw, speaker, party, result, speaker_role, sentence_text, n_words, n_words_matched, alignment_tier, alignment_source, in_debate_window.

Speaker classification heuristic: candidate names matched against per-debate roster from debates.csv; moderators matched against a curated list of 50+ panelists; audience members detected via MS./MR./DR. prefix or via a town-hall heuristic (≥7 unique speakers, ≤5 turns/speaker = audience). 91 header artifacts (e.g., SPEAKERS: lines) filtered out.

4Extract acoustic features (the voice measurements)

For every sentence, measure properties of the voice: how high or low the pitch is, how loud, how clear, how fast, how many pauses, etc.

👶 5th-grade version

Listened to every single sentence and measured the voice. Like a doctor checking your throat — but for 35,459 sentences. We measured things like:

  • 🎵 Pitch: how high or low the voice sounds
  • 🔊 Loudness: how loud they were
  • ✨ Voice clarity: clean and clear, or scratchy and rough?
  • ⏸️ Pauses: how many quiet moments and how long
  • ⚡ Speech rate: how fast they spoke

🔬 Research version

Per-sentence acoustic feature extraction via parselmouth (Praat backend) and librosa:

  • F0 (pitch): mean, std, min, max, range, slope (Hz/s); 75–500 Hz pitch ceiling, voiced frames only
  • Intensity: mean dB, std dB, range dB
  • Voice quality: local jitter, local shimmer, harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNR mean dB)
  • Spectral (librosa): spectral centroid, rolloff (85% energy), zero-crossing rate, RMS energy
  • Pauses: intensity-threshold detection at −25 dB below 90th-percentile loudness, ≥ 150 ms duration. Counts, mean duration, total duration, speech-time, speech-rate (wps), articulation-rate (wps excluding pauses)

Coverage: 33,311 / 35,459 sentences (93.9%) — non-coverage due to too-short clips (<0.3s) or parselmouth ValueErrors on edge cases.

4bAdd syllable rate & openSMILE features

Layer in syllable counts (a more accurate way to measure speaking speed than words/second) and add the openSMILE eGeMAPSv02 feature set — 88 standard voice measurements used in speech-science research.

👶 5th-grade version

Two upgrades:

1️⃣ Syllable rate: "rabbit" has 2 syllables, "I" has 1. Counting syllables tells you speed better than counting words — because "I" and "responsibility" both count as 1 word but they're very different mouthfuls.

2️⃣ openSMILE: a fancy free tool researchers use that measures 88 different voice things at once — pitch in semitones, loudness curves, vocal smoothness, formants (the shape of your mouth makes different vowel sounds). We let openSMILE measure all 35,459 sentences.

🔬 Research version

Syllable counting via syllapy (CMU dictionary lookup with vowel-group fallback for OOV words). Computed syllable_rate_per_sec (over total sentence duration) and articulation_rate_syll_per_sec (over speech-only duration), giving a finer-grained tempo measure than word rate (controls for word-length variation, e.g., "I" vs "responsibility").

openSMILE eGeMAPSv02 Functionals (Eyben et al., IEEE TAC 2016): 88 features per sentence covering semitone-scaled F0 statistics, loudness/spectral-flux functionals, jitter, shimmer, HNR, F1–F3 formant frequencies + bandwidths + relative amplitudes, alpha ratio, MFCC means, voiced-segment ratios. De facto standard for affective-computing research.

4cRoll up to per-turn metrics

Combine sentence-level measurements into per-turn measurements. A "turn" is one continuous speaking opportunity — useful for things like total floor time or pauses-per-100-words.

👶 5th-grade version

If someone gets called on and talks for 3 minutes — that's one "turn." We measured stuff at the turn level: how long they held the floor, total time spent paused, how often they paused per 100 words.

We filtered to "substantial" turns (≥10 words and ≥5 seconds) because tiny interjections like "Yes." mess up the averages.

🔬 Research version

Aggregation script joins master_sentences + acoustic_features + supplementary, then groups by (debate_audio_id, turn_id). Derived metrics include turn_floor_time_sec (end − start), turn_speech_density (speech_sec / floor_time), pauses_per_100_words, turn_speech_rate_wps (using floor time as denominator, not summed sentence-speech, to avoid pause-detection over-subtraction bias).

Filter for analysis: substantial turns (n_words ≥ 10 AND turn_floor_time_sec ≥ 5) = 1,855 candidate turns; both mean and median reported to flag outlier sensitivity.

5Extract linguistic features (the words and grammar)

For every sentence, analyze the words and grammar: sentence type, complexity, sentiment, vocabulary diversity, psycholinguistic categories, and a 384-dimensional semantic embedding.

👶 5th-grade version

Looked at the words — not just listened to the voice. For each sentence we asked:

  • 📝 Is it a statement, a question, or a command?
  • 🌳 Is the grammar simple ("I won") or complex ("Although we faced many challenges, we ultimately succeeded")?
  • 😊 Does it use happy words or angry words?
  • 🗣️ Does it say "I", "we", "you", or "they" more?
  • 🎯 What topics does it bring up — power, family, fear, patriotism?

We also turned each sentence into a magic 384-number code that captures its "meaning" — so we can later find sentences that mean similar things.

🔬 Research version

Per-sentence linguistic features via:

  • spaCy (en_core_web_sm): sentence form (declarative / interrogative / imperative / exclamatory), parse-tree depth, clause counts, subordinate clause counts (deps: ccomp/xcomp/advcl/acl/relcl), mean dependency length, negations, 13 POS-tag rates, function/content word ratios, pronoun rates (first/second/third person)
  • textstat: Flesch reading ease, Flesch-Kincaid grade, SMOG, Gunning Fog
  • VADER: lexicon-based compound, positive, negative, neutral sentiment
  • Empath (Fast et al., CHI 2016): 35 psycholinguistic categories — power, government, negative_emotion, aggression, optimism, pride, certainty, achievement, patriotism, war, family, religion, etc.
  • Sentence-BERT (all-MiniLM-L6-v2): 384-dim sentence embeddings, normalized; stored as parquet for embedding-centroid analyses

Output: linguistic_features.csv 35,459 × 76 + linguistic_embeddings.parquet 35,459 × 385.

5bTag discourse acts & bias

For every sentence, tag what discourse act it performs (statement, agreement, rejection, command, etc.) AND whether it contains specific bias patterns (fear appeal, patriotism, personal attack, etc.). Multi-label, rule-based.

👶 5th-grade version

Gave each sentence labels for what kind of thing it does. One sentence might get many labels at once:

  • "I agree" → label: AGREEMENT
  • "No, that's not true!" → labels: REJECTION
  • "If we don't act now, disaster!" → labels: FEAR APPEAL
  • "Look, we made America stronger." → labels: STATEMENT + PATRIOTISM

This lets us count, for example, how many times winners vs losers used fear appeals or made personal attacks.

🔬 Research version

Rule-based multi-label tagger over the BEADS-TAGS schema (extends DAMSL with project-specific bias categories). 23 discourse acts + 21 bias categories implemented as Python regex/keyword detectors. Detectors combine:

  • Sentence-form cues (from stage 5 spaCy classification)
  • Auxiliary-verb / wh-word patterns for question type
  • Lexical patterns for greetings, apologies, thanks, acknowledgments
  • Stance markers ("I believe...") for personal-stance bias
  • First-person achievement verbs ("I built / created / rebuilt...") for self-promotion bias
  • Named-entity + blame-verb combinations for attribution

Six tags marked text-untracked (gender bias, cognitive bias, gender dismissal, issue avoidance, selective evidence, cultural bias) require contextual or world-knowledge reasoning beyond rule-based detection — reserved for future human/LLM annotation.

6Compare winners vs losers (the analysis)

Test every feature we extracted to see which ones reliably differ between winners and losers. Use proper statistics with per-debate controls and multiple-comparison correction.

👶 5th-grade version

Now the fun part. We took everything we measured and asked: does this differ between winners and losers?

To be fair, we did three things:

  1. For each measurement, compare winners vs losers across all 26 debates
  2. Control for which debate it came from (so Trump's voice doesn't unfairly dominate 4 debates)
  3. Correct for the fact that we tested LOTS of measurements (otherwise some would look "significant" just by random chance)

We also trained a "winner detector" using only the voice (no words) — to see if winners sound different.

🔬 Research version

Three analyses:

A. Univariate effects + per-debate fixed effects. For each of 39 candidate features, fit OLS model: feat ~ is_winner + C(debate_audio_id). Report Cohen's d, Mann-Whitney U p-value, fixed-effects coefficient + p-value. Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the 39 tests at α=0.05. 28 features survive correction.

B. LDA on openSMILE eGeMAPSv02. 88-feature Linear Discriminant Analysis, candidate sentences only. Reported: training accuracy, 5-fold random-split CV (overoptimistic), and leave-one-debate-out CV (honest estimate, controls for speaker confounds). Result: 0.681 ± 0.041 LODOCV.

C. Embedding-based semantic comparison. Compute centroids μ_winner, μ_loser in 384-dim sentence-BERT space; direction vector d = μ_winner − μ_loser; project every sentence onto d; report most "winner-direction" and most "loser-direction" sentences.

★Headline findings

Voice clarity (HNR)

+0.24
Winners have clearer voices. Cohen's d, p < 10⁻¹⁷³. The single strongest discriminator.

Pitch variability

−0.17
Winners have less pitch variation — more controlled prosody. p < 10⁻⁶⁰.

Pause time

+0.17
Winners pause more (0.40s vs 0.29s per sentence). p < 10⁻⁴⁴.

Subordinate clauses

+0.09
Winners use more syntactically complex sentences.

Patriotism appeals

−0.04
Losers invoke patriotic language more often, not less. Counter-intuitive.

Audio-only winner detector

68%
LDA on 88 openSMILE features predicts winner vs loser at 68% accuracy in leave-one-debate-out CV (chance = 50%).

The pattern: winners project authority through controlled phonation and deliberate pausing rather than through volume, pitch elevation, or patriotic language. They construct grammatically more complex sentences while keeping vocabulary at the same level of accessibility (Flesch ~72 for both groups). Linguistic findings often run against folk intuition — losers, not winners, are the heavier users of patriotism, fear appeals, and historical references.
